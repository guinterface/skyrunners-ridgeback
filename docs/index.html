Inside the drone

Our own 2.24 m VTOL drone for measuring how much water chaparral shrubs lose between dawn and midday, for planning prescribed burns. Click any part on the drone or in the list to see what it does, why we chose it, what it connects to and where to buy it.

Power

Battery, power module and servo regulator: 648 Wh at 21.6 V, about 56 A in a hot hover.

Lift (hover)

Four motors, props and speed controllers that lift the drone off a hillside and land it.

Cruise (pusher)

The pusher that flies the drone on the wing over the plot.

Flight control

Flight controller, GPS, airspeed, radios and Remote ID.

Control surfaces

Four servos for the ailerons and the arch tail.

Airframe

Printed wing, fuselage and tail on carbon tubes. Takeoff mass 8.71 kg.

Mission sensors

What the mission needs: thermal camera, 1610 nm spot sensor, colour camera, air sensor and the computer that logs them. 230 g, 10 W.

Ground equipment

Stays on the ground: the safety pilot's transmitter and the battery charger (the laptop radio comes with the telemetry pair).

With fairings and a 12 min survey the field day fits on one charge (3.6 flights); loiter reaches 72 of 80 min. 80 min is possible only if fairings remove most of that drag (the upper bound is 91 min). A bigger battery is not the way out: a 6S7P pack drops hot-day hover thrust below 1.5 times the weight (Battery_Options). The next real step is to measure: weigh the printed parts, bench-test the pusher and fit the drag from the first flights, since every row here rests on an estimated drag.

What the drone carries for the mission

The parts above only make the drone fly. The mission is to measure how much water each shrub loses between dawn and midday. That needs three readings over the same plants on every flight, and each one comes from a different sensor:

  1. Is the plant stressed? Leaves that run short of water close their pores and warm up compared with the air: the thermal camera, read against the air sensor.
  2. How much water do the leaves hold? Water absorbs short-wave infrared light near 1610 nm: the spot sensor, best paired with its 850 nm channel, which water does not absorb (the pair gives a leaf-water index; $214, still to decide).
  3. Which plant is it, and how much of each reading is leaf and how much is soil? The colour camera. Its overlapping photos also give each shrub's 3D shape, so no separate 3D sensor is needed.

That is the minimum: drop the thermal camera, the spot sensor or the colour camera and one of the three questions has no answer. The air sensor on the drone adds the air at flight height (the main air reference is the weather meter on the ground), and the computer and the 5 V regulator make it all work. A SWIR camera or centimetre GPS would make the readings better; see "Not decided yet" below. Click a row to see the sensor on the drone.

What we would lose without each one

SensorWithout itEvidence
Thermal cameraNo measure of plant stress: leaf temperature against air temperature is the only stress signal the drone has. The papers below are on irrigated crops and orchards, not chaparral; how well it works over shrubs is part of what the first flights test.
  • Jackson et al., Water Resources Research 17:1133 (1981): canopy temperature as a water stress indicator (the crop water stress index)
  • Berni et al., IEEE Trans. Geoscience and Remote Sensing 47:722 (2009): a small drone with an inexpensive thermal camera (40 cm pixels) mapped water stress from canopy temperature, checked on the ground
  • Gonzalez-Dugo et al., Precision Agriculture 14:660 (2013): drone thermal images used to map the water status of five tree species in one orchard
  • Aragon et al., Sensors 20:3316 (2020): calibrating a FLIR A655sc pixel by pixel against a lab black body cut its error from about 6 °C to under 1 °C; not tested on a Lepton
Air temperature and humidityThe weather meter on the ground still gives the air near the canopy, so stress can still be read. What is lost is the air at flight height, which at dawn can be several degrees different because of the morning temperature inversion.
  • Idso et al., Agricultural Meteorology 24:45 (1981): leaf-minus-air temperature has to be normalised for the weather, mainly how dry the air is, before it means stress
1610 nm spot sensorWe would see stress (leaf pores closing) but have no reading tied to how much water the leaves hold. The band responds to water per leaf area; live fuel moisture, which fire planners use, is water per dry mass, so the two are related, not the same. Open risk: the change between dawn and midday may be too small for this band to see; the first flights test that.
  • Hunt and Rock, Remote Sensing of Environment 30:43 (1989): changes in leaf water content detected with near- and middle-infrared reflectance
  • Ceccato et al., Remote Sensing of Environment 77:22 (2001): detecting leaf water content from reflectance in the optical bands
  • Yebra et al., Remote Sensing of Environment 136:455 (2013): review of remote sensing of live fuel moisture for fire danger
Near-infrared reference channelThe 1610 nm reading would also change with lighting, shadow and how much of the 21 m spot is soil, not only with leaf water.
  • Hunt and Rock, Remote Sensing of Environment 30:43 (1989): changes in leaf water content detected with near- and middle-infrared reflectance
  • Gao, Remote Sensing of Environment 58:257 (1996): a water index from two near-infrared bands (860 and 1240 nm), one absorbed by water and one not
Colour cameraWe could not find the same shrub at dawn and at midday, or tell leaf from soil. At midday bare soil is far hotter than leaves, so a thermal pixel that mixes in soil looks like a stressed shrub.
  • Dandois and Ellis, Remote Sensing of Environment 136:259 (2013): 3D canopy structure from overlapping photos taken with an ordinary camera on a hobby drone below 130 m

Not decided yet

  • A SWIR camera instead of the spot sensor: images of every shrub instead of one reading per 21 m spot. $2,250 a week to rent, $5,650 and up to buy; asked several companies.
  • The near-infrared channel in the table above (850 nm, $214): recommended before the first mission flights: it turns the 1610 nm reading into a leaf-water index that lighting and shadow affect much less.
  • A vegetation layer on sheet 1 showing where the chaparral actually is, to place the plot.
  • Centimetre GPS (RTK) to find the same shrub on every flight without ground targets. About $3,000; later.

How the electronics are held, and how you reach them

Everything sits on printed plates screwed into heat-set inserts, so parts can be moved without new holes, and every link between groups is a plug (JST-GH for signals, XT30/XT60/XT90 for power), so a group can come out without cutting or soldering.
